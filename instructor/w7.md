## Preparation

- One printed set per team; keep cards 1–3 back until the start of units 26–28.
- A visible clock for game time; the game master announces time jumps.
- Brief the guests: their role is to comment, not to give legal advice; every local question stays *open* with
  the responsible role.

## Typical mistakes

- Treating the AI Act as the only relevant law; the MDR (and, for in-vitro diagnostics, the IVDR) apply alongside.
- Classifying by technology ("it is AI") instead of by intended purpose.
- Stating that high-risk obligations "apply now". After Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, Chapter III Sections 1–3 apply
  from 2 August 2028 for systems under Article 6(1) and Annex I, and from 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems.
  Article 4 (AI literacy) is in Chapter I and has applied since 2 February 2025, in amended form since 27 July 2026.
- Merging Article 33 and Article 34 GDPR into one decision; starting the 72-hour clock when the ticket was created
  rather than when the controller became aware.
- Calling logs with pseudonyms, age, arrival time and triage text "anonymised".
- Deleting the ticket "to limit the damage" before evidence is secured.
- A control without a test.

## Model answers

**Unit 25 (regulatory note, dated).** Purpose A: aggregated retrospective statistics for research, no individual
decisions; on its wording not a medical device purpose (MDR Article 2(1) and (12)). Purpose B: per-patient alert
prompting a second review before discharge, i.e. information used for diagnostic decisions about individuals; a
candidate for medical device software, Rule 11 points to class IIa at least (IIb if decisions could lead to serious
deterioration or surgery). Manufacturer: the software company (places it on the market / puts it into service under
its name); the hospital is deployer under the AI Act (Article 3) and user under the MDR. If purpose B qualifies and
requires notified-body assessment, the system is high-risk under AI Act Article 6(1) with Annex I; obligations of
Chapter III Sections 1–3 apply from 2 August 2028. Missing manufacturer document, for example: an intended-purpose
statement and instructions for use for purpose B, a clinical evaluation for purpose B, or evidence of conformity
assessment. *Open:* final classification (manufacturer's regulatory affairs); whether purpose B could be pursued
in a clinical investigation instead (study lead with ethics committee).

Variant for discussion: if the hospital itself developed the software for purpose B, the in-house conditions of MDR
Article 5(5) would have to be examined; whether such an in-house device meets condition (b) of AI Act Article 6(1)
(third-party conformity assessment required) is a question of interpretation and stays *open* for legal counsel.

**Unit 26.** Care flow: purpose B, Article 6(1) with Article 9(2)(h) GDPR, recipients ED clinicians and the company
as processor (Article 28 contract), deletion of request logs after a short, fixed period. Research flow: separate
purpose, Article 6(1) with Article 9(2)(j) and Article 89, based on Member State law (for example § 27 BDSG or a
state law; which one is *open*), pseudonymised extract, access for the research team only. Section 7 of the draft
contract is wrong: the logs are personal data (pseudonym, age, arrival time and free text). DPIA pre-check:
large-scale processing of health data (Article 35(3)(b)) and new technology suggest that a DPIA is required.
Decision: do not sign in this form; require log minimisation (no free text), deletion periods, sub-processor list
and a third-country check (Chapter V) if support staff work outside the EU.

**Unit 27.** Confidentiality: logs without free text and with pseudonyms only; test: send a synthetic request with a
name in the free text and show that the log contains a placeholder; role: product owner, checked by information
security. Integrity: approved-version list with checksums, service refuses to start otherwise; test: install a
modified model file on the test system; role: information security with steering board. Availability: documented
fall-back to usual reading without alert; test: switch off the alert in a drill and check that discharges still
follow the regular reading; role: clinical lead. Decision at 08:30: switch the alert off (or back to 2.2.0 only after
verifying its checksum) until the update has been assessed.

**Unit 28.** Containment: revoke support access to tickets with attachments, stop log export. Evidence: secure the
ticket, access logs and version records; record checksums. Alerting: information security → data protection
officer → hospital management as controller; clinical lead; the company confirms in writing (Article 33(2)).
Assessment: 40 pseudonymised entries with triage notes and one name with date of birth, visible to 12 support staff
for three weeks. Article 33: the controller became aware on Day 30 at 10:40; notification without undue delay and,
where feasible, within 72 hours (by Day 33, 10:40) unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk. Health data
and a direct identifier make a risk likely. Article 34: whether a high risk is likely depends on the content of the
triage note and the trustworthiness of the recipients; document the reasoning either way. Restart: approved version
verified by checksum, logs minimised, support access restricted, synthetic test case reproduced, sign-off by
clinical lead and information security. Separate question: whether the unapproved update is a reportable event
under the MDR depends on the legal status of the product (*open*; purpose B is not authorised).
